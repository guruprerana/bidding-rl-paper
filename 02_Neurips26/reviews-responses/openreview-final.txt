Auction-Based Online Policy Adaptation for Evolving Objectives

Abstract:
We consider multi-objective reinforcement learning problems where objectives come from an identical family---such as the class of reachability objectives---and may appear or disappear at runtime. Our goal is to design adaptive policies that can efficiently adjust their behaviors as the set of active objectives changes. To solve this problem, we propose a modular framework where each objective is supported by a selfish local policy, and coordination is achieved through a novel auction-based mechanism: policies bid for the right to execute their actions, with bids reflecting the urgency of the current state. The highest bidder selects the action, enabling a dynamic and interpretable trade-off among objectives. Going back to the original adaptation problem, when objectives change, the system adapts by simply adding or removing the corresponding policies. Moreover, as objectives arise from the same family, identical copies of a parameterized policy can be deployed, facilitating immediate adaptation at runtime. We show how the selfish local policies can be computed by turning the problem into a general-sum Markov game, where the policies compete against each other to fulfill their own objectives. To succeed, each policy must not only optimize its own objective, but also reason about the presence of other goals and learn to produce calibrated bids that reflect relative priority. Under mild assumptions, we prove the existence of Nash equilibria where dishonest bidding leads to suboptimal outcome, and the most urgent objectives win control automatically. In our implementation, the policies are trained concurrently using proximal policy optimization (PPO). We evaluate on two Atari games and a gridworld-based path-planning task with dynamic targets. Our method achieves substantially better performance than monolithic policies trained with PPO.

Metareview:
The paper considers environments where the active set of goals changes during deployment. Instead of training one policy to jointly optimise every possible combination, it assigns each objective a local policy and lets these policies compete for control through learned bids. A pooling mechanism allows a policy to account for a variable number of concurrent goals. Theoretical results analyse desirable bidding behaviour, while experiments suggest that the modular system adapts more effectively than the reported monolithic PPO baselines.

Reviewers acknowledge that the auction mechanism is original, interpretable and naturally suited to changing objective sets. The modular design and theoretical treatment are appealing.

However, the reviewers have pointed out several issues, centered around the adequacy of the baseline comparisons, interpretation of the learned bidding behaviour, and the breadth and scalability of the evaluation.

Apart from the other reviewer comments, please respond to the following issues:

Please address Reviewers HLic and C6kf’s concerns regarding the strength of the centralised comparisons, particularly the absence of an urgency-weighted scalarisation baseline.
Please address Reviewer HLic’s concern regarding why the non-pooled Winner-Pays method outperforms the pooled variant.
Please address Reviewers MWV4 and C6kf’s concerns regarding generalisation beyond closely related reachability objectives and to larger objective sets.
Please address Reviewers MWV4 and C6kf’s concerns regarding the assumption that objectives are known and separable by clearly defining the intended problem setting.
Final Justification:
The authors addressed the main concerns raised in the initial meta-review. The centralised baseline, clarification of the role of pooling, and additional results in a more heterogeneous environment have provided more clarity about the approach and its generalisation performance. While the empirical evaluation is still limited in breadth, the main contribution is not undermined by it. Overall, the paper presents an interesting and technically sound approach to adapting policies under changing objectives with improved empirical justifications after rebuttal. I therefore recommend the paper for acceptance.

In the ablation without attention pooling, the policies still observe all the objectives and are not “blind”. The ablation only removes the attention pooling module in the policy network. We will explicitly clarify this in the final draft.

Nonetheless, to address the reviewer’s concern, we agree that Table 1 can give the impression that removing pooling improves Winner-Pays. 3. Generalizability beyond reachability objectives
The auction interface itself does not require all objectives to share the same reward function or policy parameters. Heterogeneous objectives can be incorporated by training objective-specific local policies while retaining the same auction mechanism. What is lost relative to the homogeneous setting is the ability to deploy arbitrary copies of a single shared policy without additional training.

Implementation of a Cat Feeder env. variant with heterogenous objectives

To directly address the question of generalizability, we implement a more complex variant of the Cat Feeder env. in which each cat has a different priority for feeding such that feeding a cat with higher priority yields a larger reward. Additionally, the robot has a limited battery that needs to be recharged at charging stations before it runs out. There are 8 cats and 3 charging stations all of which are moving stochastically in a 30x30 gridworld.

We use our auction-based approach with one policy for each cat along with an additional policy responsible for charging the robot before it dies. We encode the safety-critical status of battery charging through a larger bid cap, illustrating one simple mechanism for incorporating known objective-level priorities.

Training and evaluation is performed as in the paper. We report the results here, where the priority score is the sum of the priorities of the cats that have been successfully fed, ranging from 1 to 4 with 4 being the highest priority.

4. Separability of objectives
Explicit separation of objectives is a standard assumption in multi-objective reinforcement learning in which we have access to the reward components corresponding to each objective [13,30,31,22,...]. There are many practical scenarios when this is reasonable. For instance, in factory settings where robots need to perform a series of manufacturing tasks [A]. As a second example, consider the traveling salesman problem, which requires multiple target vertices to be visited (i.e., multiple objectives) on a given graph, with clearly marked targets. Needless to mention, the traveling salesmen problem has numerous variants and many applications in computer science.

We agree that situations where the objectives are not separately given is also interesting; we will consider this in future works.

Summary
This paper addresses a fundamental bottleneck in Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning (MORL): adapting to a highly dynamic, fluctuating number of objectives at runtime. Standard centralized PPO policies fail in these environments due to the combinatorial explosion of the state space and severe credit assignment issues. To solve this, the authors propose a modular, decentralized architecture where independent local policies handle navigation, while a separate bidding network utilizes an Attention Pooling "radar" to evaluate global urgency. Control of the agent is then dynamically allocated via an auction mechanism (Winner-Pays or All-Pay) governed by a bid penalty. This structural decoupling of "who controls" from "how to act" successfully enables zero-shot transfer to environments with significantly higher objective counts than seen during training.

Contribution Type: General: Most submissions will fall into this type.
Strengths And Weaknesses:
Strengths
Elegant Solution to the Credit Assignment Problem: By abandoning global linear scalarization (the "monolithic brain" approach), the framework fundamentally sidesteps the noisy gradient updates that plague standard RL in dense environments. Local policies only receive reward signals for their specific assigned target, making the mathematical feedback loop for movement dynamics incredibly clean and sample-efficient.
Zero-Shot Scalability via Attention Pooling: The inclusion of the Attention Pooling module bridges the gap between fixed-architecture neural networks and dynamic environments. By squashing an arbitrary number of targets into a fixed-size embedding, the system breaks the rigid input-layer constraints of standard PPO, allowing a model trained on 8 objectives to flawlessly execute in a 14-objective environment without retraining.
High Interpretability: Unlike the "black box" nature of a centralized reward sum, the auction framework is highly transparent. Researchers can directly observe the bid distributions to understand exactly how the agent is prioritizing tasks in real-time.
Theoretical Rigor & Incentive Alignment: The theoretical formulation of the problem and the subsequent analysis are commendable. The auction mechanism, specifically the Winner-Pays setting combined with a bid penalty, elegantly solves the Nash equilibrium problem among competing sub-policies.
Weaknesses
The major weaknesses of the paper are twofold, primarily concerning the theoretical justification of the ablation studies and the choice of baselines for the fully observable setting.

1. The Theoretical & Empirical Disconnect in the Absence of Pooling
Consider the ablation case where attention pooling is removed. Without this module, policies are effectively blind to the global state and cannot estimate their competitors' urgencies. Assuming a discrete bidding range of 0 to 6 and 8 competing policies, consider the following dynamics:

If a policy bids a middle value (e.g., 3), the probability of winning the auction (assuming competitors randomly distribute their bids or tie-break) is extremely low. For instance, the probability of all 7 other policies bidding 3 or lower is roughly 
%, meaning the probability of losing is 
% (Simplified calculation to demonstrate the overall idea.).
In the All-Pay setting, this near-certainty of losing while still paying the penalty mathematically forces the policies to bid 0 to avoid bleeding points. This perfectly explains the poor, highly variant (high standard deviation) empirical performance shown for All-Pay without pooling.
In the Winner-Pays setting, however, losing the auction costs nothing. If a policy's target is extremely urgent, it must bid the maximum value to have any chance of winning. If the target is safe, it bids 0. Theoretically, this should result in a highly polarized, bimodal bid distribution (0 or Max) where multiple blind policies frequently tie at the maximum bid during concurrent emergencies, leading to random tie-breaking.
The Discrepancy: Theoretically, this blind, tie-breaking approach should yield suboptimal coordination compared to the globally aware pooled method. Yet, the empirical results (Table 1) show superior and stable (Less standard deviation) performance for the non-pooled Winner-Pays model. It is unclear why an empirically blind, bimodal strategy outperforms the theoretically superior pooled coordination on the training set.
2. Absence of a Strong Urgency-Weighted Baseline
When attention pooling is active, the global state and all task urgencies are fully observable to the system. Therefore, comparing this to a simple linear scalarization (an unweighted global reward sum) is not entirely fair. A much stronger, standard MORL baseline for a fully observable setting would be dynamic linear scalarization, where the reward components are weighted by their normalized urgency:

1. Quantifying the ease of credit assignment
While it is hard to quantify the credit assignment other than through the performance of the policies, a concrete example can clarify the credit assignment advantage with the multi-controller approach: if two objectives are located on opposite sides, then the distance-based reward shaping terms would cancel each other out when training a single policy with a sum of rewards. But the separate rewards are maintained with the multi-policy training. More generally, the centralized policy must infer both which objective should currently be pursued and which action advances it from a single scalar return. Our method separates these roles: local policies learn objective-specific actions, while the auction learns control allocation.

2. Explicit knowledge of various objectives
Explicit separation of multiple objectives/rewards is a standard assumption in the vast literature of multi-objective reinforcement learning in which we have access to the reward components corresponding to each objective [13,30,31,22,...]. There are many practical scenarios when this is reasonable. For instance, in factory settings where robots need to perform a series of manufacturing tasks [A]. As a second example, consider the traveling salesman problem, which requires multiple target vertices to be visited (i.e., multiple objectives) on a given graph, with clearly marked targets. Needless to mention, the traveling salesmen problem has numerous variants and many applications in computer science.

Contradictory Ablation Results (Winner-Pays): The paper theoretically motivates providing global observations to achieve "environment-aware bidding" (Challenge II, Lines 83-89) and claims the ablation studies validate the attention pooling architecture (Lines 488-490).
